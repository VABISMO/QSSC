Quantum Solid State Chip (Room Temperature) — Fabrication Protocol

Project: QSSC (Quantum Solid State Chip, room-temperature demonstrator) Document type: Laboratory fabrication protocol (standalone) Version: 1.0 Scope: Home / maker-lab demonstrator integrating (1) an electrically-pumped carbon-quantum-dot (CQD) ASE/LED emitter array, (2) TEOS sol-gel silica (SiO₂) waveguides — chosen over SU-8 for negligible autofluorescence under the 532 nm pump, essential for reading the weak ODMR PL — and (3) a PMMA-coated hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) layer hosting optically-addressable spin defects (negatively-charged boron vacancy, VB−, zero-field splitting D ≈ 3.46 GHz, plus carbon-related centers), with on-chip 2D-material (graphene/MoS₂) photodetector readout and Peltier thermal management, operating at room temperature.

Note on the emitter claim. True electrically-pumped lasing from carbon quantum dots is not claimed in this protocol, and the reader should not infer it from the literature cited. What is targeted and demonstrated here is electroluminescence (EL) and amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) / high-brightness edge emission from a CQD–polymer composite diode stack, used as a compact optical pump and on-chip source. ASE is a necessary but not sufficient condition for lasing; achieving a coherent laser cavity with CQDs requires a resonator with sufficient gain and mode confinement that is outside the scope of this demonstrator. See the Validation section for the precise boundary of what is supported by the literature.

1. Abstract

This document specifies a complete, equipment-minimal fabrication protocol for a room-temperature solid-state quantum photonic chip (QSSC). The chip combines three functional layers on a single 25 × 25 mm² carrier: (i) a hydrothermally-synthesized carbon quantum dot (CQD) emitter array embedded in a polymeric LED/ASE stack that provides electrically-pumped broadband visible electroluminescence centered near 450–620 nm; (ii) a liquid-phase-exfoliated and chemically-engineered hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) thin film, spin-coated with PMMA, hosting optically-addressable spin defects including the negatively-charged boron vacancy (VB−) with a zero-field splitting D ≈ 3.46 GHz and carbon-related color centers; and (iii) TEOS sol-gel silica (SiO₂) waveguides that route pump and defect fluorescence to an on-chip 2D-material (graphene / MoS₂) photodetector array. A two-stage Peltier module holds the hBN layer at 288–300 K for stable room-temperature operation and ODMR. All processing steps use only benchtop chemistry, a spin coater, a hotplate, a low-cost mask-aligner or contact-mask UV exposure, a thermal evaporator (or commercial sputter coater), a CNC/3D-printed alignment jig, and a ~USD 3,500 total equipment footprint beyond an optical microscope. The protocol gives times, temperatures, concentrations, spin speeds, layer thicknesses, and RF frequencies at every step, and validates each major claim against peer-reviewed literature.

2. Introduction & Scientific Basis

2.1 Why a room-temperature quantum chip demonstrator

Most solid-state spin-qubit platforms (NV centers in diamond, GaAs quantum dots, superconducting qubits) require cryogenic operation (< 10 K) or specialized microwave hardware. Optically-addressable spin defects in hexagonal boron nitride are a recent and notable exception: the negatively-charged boron vacancy, VB−, exhibits a spin-1 triplet with zero-field splitting D ≈ 3.46 GHz, optical initialization and readout, and measurable spin coherence at room temperature. This makes hBN a credible substrate for a maker-lab quantum sensor demonstrator.

2.2 Why carbon quantum dots as the on-chip source

Carbon quantum dots (CQDs, also called carbon nanodots, C-dots) are fluorescent carbon nanoparticles (2–10 nm) synthesized by bottom-up hydrothermal carbonization of citric acid + nitrogen dopants (e.g., urea, ethylenediamine). They are aqueous, low-toxicity, cheap, and emit across the visible spectrum with quantum yields of 40–80 % in solution. When embedded in a thin electroluminescent polymer host and driven by a modest DC bias (~5–12 V), they produce broadband EL suitable as a compact pump for nearby defect centers. We do not claim a CQD laser; we claim an electrically-pumped CQD LED/ASE source.

2.3 Why polymer waveguides and 2D-material photodetectors

TEOS sol-gel silica (SiO₂) is the waveguide material here: fluorescence-free, UV–NIR transparent, densified at 300–400 °C, and patterned by soft-lithography / UV embossing — all compatible with benchtop processing. It is chosen over organic photoresists (e.g. SU-8) because its negligible autofluorescence under the 532 nm pump is essential for reading the weak ODMR PL. Graphene and MoS₂ photodetectors offer broadband absorption, gate-tunable responsivity, and integration directly on the waveguide facet — eliminating discrete photodiodes and enabling on-chip readout of ODMR fluorescence.

Section 1 — CQD Emitter Array

Goal: Synthesize water-soluble, nitrogen-doped CQDs and integrate them into a patterned ITO/PEDOT:PSS/CQD–PVA/ZnO/Ag electroluminescent diode array (8 × 8 pixels, 1 mm² each; ZnO is the electron-transport layer, Ag the top cathode) on a 25 × 25 mm² ITO-glass substrate. For this chip the CQDs are synthesised green (~520–540 nm) so the on-chip emitter doubles as the optical pump for the VB− defect (no external pump laser needed for the end-to-end demo). Target: EL peak 520–540 nm (green), turn-on 4–8 V, radiance ≥ 10 cd/m², ASE threshold detectable under electrical overdrive.

1.1 Hydrothermal synthesis of N-doped CQDs

This follows the citric-acid + urea hydrothermal route, a well-established "bottom-up" CQD synthesis.

  1. Charge. In a 50 mL PTFE-lined autoclave, dissolve 1.0 g citric acid + 1.0 g urea in 10 mL DI water. Stir 5 min until clear. No ethylenediamine is required: the emission colour is set mainly by the hydrothermal temperature (see step 6). For this chip we want green CQDs (~520–540 nm) to pump the VB−, so use the high end of the temperature range (~220 °C).
  2. Hydrothermal. Seal autoclave, place in hotplate oven preheated to 180 °C (blue CQDs, ~450–470 nm) or 220 °C (green CQDs, ~520–540 nm — the choice for this chip to pump VB−), hold 6–10 h (longer time at the higher temperature also red-shifts). (Internal pressure ~10–15 bar autogenous.) Cooling: remove from hotplate, allow to cool to < 60 °C naturally (~2 h) before opening.
  3. Resulting product: dark-brown solution containing CQDs (2–6 nm), unreacted precursor, larger carbonaceous particles.
  4. Purification. - Centrifuge at 10,000 rpm (≈ 9,000 × g) for 15 min; discard the large-particle pellet. - Filter supernatant through 0.22 µm PVDF syringe filter. - Dialyze against DI water (MWCO 1000 Da) for 48 h, changing water every 8 h, to remove small molecules. - Yield: typically 20–40 mg of purified CQDs per batch (depends on dialysis losses), consistent with typical citric-acid/urea yields reported in the literature.
  5. Characterization (benchtop). Dilute 1:1000 in water; record PL under 365 nm UV lamp — bright blue-green emission. Approximate quantum yield by comparing against quinine sulfate standard in 0.1 M H₂SO₄ (QY ≈ 54 % typical). Optional TEM drop-cast for size.
  6. Wavelength tuning (temperature is the primary knob). With citric acid + urea the emission colour is set mainly by the hydrothermal temperature (and reaction time): ~180 °C → blue (~450–470 nm), ~200 °C → blue-green (~480–500 nm), ~220 °C / longer time → green (~520–540 nm). The mechanism is a larger sp² carbon core + heavier nitrogen doping + denser surface states at higher temperature → smaller effective bandgap → red shift. (Ethylenediamine as a dopant can push further to 540–620 nm but is not required to reach green.) For this chip, synthesise green CQDs (~220 °C) so the on-chip emitter pumps the VB− directly around the 532 nm excitation band — no external pump laser is needed for the end-to-end demonstration.

1.2 CQD–polymer electroluminescent ink

  1. Composite. Dissolve 200 mg PVA in 4 mL DI water at 90 °C, stir 1 h, cool. Add 20 mg CQD powder (or 2 mL of concentrated CQD solution), sonicate 15 min, stir 1 h. Final CQD loading ≈ 10 wt% in 5 wt% PVA. Filter 0.45 µm.
  2. Optional ASE-lean grade: For higher film uniformity, add 5 wt% urea (relative to PVA) as plasticizer; this reduces cracking and raises the effective optical gain length.

1.3 Substrate preparation (ITO glass)

  1. Cut ITO glass to 25 × 25 mm² (or use pre-cut). Pattern ITO anode by masking with tape / photoresist and etching in HCl (37 %):HNO₃ (65 %):H₂O = 1:1:5, 50 °C, 5 min. Stop in DI rinse.
  2. Clean: sonicate 10 min each in detergent, DI water, acetone, IPA. UV-ozone 15 min (or piranha if available).
  3. Define 8 × 8 pixel array (1 mm² pads, 0.5 mm gap) using a laser-printed transparency mask on the ITO side.

1.4 PEDOT:PSS hole-injection layer

  1. Filter PEDOT:PSS (AI 4083) through 0.45 µm PVDF. Spin-coat on ITO at 3000 rpm, 30 s → ~40 nm. Bake 120 °C, 15 min on hotplate.

1.5 CQD–PVA emissive layer

  1. Spin-coat CQD–PVA ink at 2000 rpm, 40 s → ~80 nm. (Thicker films for ASE: 1500 rpm, 60 s → ~120–150 nm.) Bake 80 °C, 20 min under vacuum desiccator to remove residual water (water kills EL).
  2. ASE waveguide geometry: to favor in-plane ASE, deposit a second, thicker (~250 nm) neat-CQD strip on the pixel edge by drop-casting through a shadow mask (0.5 mm wide) and slow drying at 60 °C. This forms the gain strip into which the diode EL is edge-coupled.

1.5b ZnO electron-transport layer (ETL)

14b. ZnO ETL. Disperse ZnO nanopowder (~20–40 nm particles; ball-mill coarser ZnO if needed) in ethanol or butanol at 20 mg/mL, sonicate 30 min, filter 0.45 µm. Spin-coat on the CQD–PVA layer at 3000 rpm, 30 s → ~30–50 nm ETL. Bake 150 °C, 10 min on hotplate. ZnO (bandgap 3.37 eV ≈ 368 nm) is transparent to both the green pump and the 850 nm defect PL; it injects electrons into the CQD layer and blocks holes, completing the p-i-n stack opposite the PEDOT:PSS hole-transport layer.

1.6 Top cathode

  1. Top cathode (Ag). Two routes:
    • Maker route (no evaporator — recommended here): dispense conductive silver paste from a syringe (the type sold for circuit-track repair) through an 8 × 8 shadow mask, doctor-blade / spread to ~1–2 µm, dry/cure per the paste spec (typically 60–80 °C, 15 min, or air-cure 2 h). Silver-paste cathodes are higher-resistance and thicker than evaporated films but entirely adequate for a demonstrator and need no vacuum equipment.
    • Lab route: thermal evaporate Ag (or Al) through a shadow mask, 100 nm, rate 0.5–1 Å/s, base pressure < 5 × 10⁻⁶ mbar. A home sputter coater at ~10⁻³ mbar with a mask gives adequate but higher-resistivity cathodes. Pattern 8 × 8 matching pads on the ZnO ETL.
  2. Encapsulate: spin 200 nm PMMA on top as moisture barrier; cure 120 °C, 10 min.

1.7 Electrical drive and EL check

  1. Contact ITO anode with Cu tape, cathode with probe needle. Drive with DC bias; increase from 0 V. Turn-on expected 4–8 V; visible EL under dim room light by ~10 V. Drive current 2–20 mA per pixel.
  2. ASE probe: drive a pixel at 2× threshold current; collect edge emission through a 600 µm fiber into a compact USB spectrometer (Ocean Optics-style). Look for spectral narrowing (FWHM drop from ~80 nm to < 25 nm) above threshold — signature of ASE, not lasing.

Timing summary, Section 1: synthesis 8 h + purification 48 h dialysis (mostly unattended) + film stack 1 day + metal deposition half day. Total: ≈ 3 working days.

Section 2 — hBN Exfoliation, Defect Engineering, PMMA Coating

Goal: Produce a thin (~2–4 nm, 3–8 layer) hBN film on SiO₂/Si or glass, generate VB− defects (D ≈ 3.46 GHz) at controllable density (~0.1–0.3 /µm²), and cap with a 50 nm PMMA passivation/protection layer.

2.1 Liquid-phase exfoliation of hBN

  1. Disperse. Add 100 mg hBN powder to 20 mL NMP in a 20 mL vial. NMP is a known good solvent for BN because its surface tension (~40 mN/m) matches the exfoliation energy of layered materials.
  2. Sonication. Bath sonicate 12 h at 40 kHz, 200 W, water bath maintained < 35 °C (ice replacement every 2 h). Tip sonication (60 % amplitude, 2 h, on/off 3 s/1 s) gives higher yield if available.
  3. Size selection. Centrifuge at 1500 rpm (~200 × g), 20 min; collect top 70 % supernatant (thin flakes). Re-centrifuge supernatant at 4000 rpm (~1500 × g), 20 min; collect top 50 % — this is the few-layer fraction (typical lateral size 50–300 nm, thickness 2–6 nm by AFM).
  4. Wash. Dilute 1:3 with IPA, centrifuge 8000 rpm 10 min, redisperse in IPA. Repeat 2× to remove NMP.
  5. Concentrate. Redisperse in IPA at 1 mg/mL. Characterize by drop-casting on SiO₂/Si and AFM (target 3–8 layers).
  6. Yield: ~5–15 % of starting mass as few-layer flakes; consistent with literature yields for bath sonication of BN in NMP.

2.2 Film deposition

  1. Substrate: 5 × 5 mm² Si (300 nm SiO₂) or glass, cleaned (Sec. 1.10), UV-ozone 15 min.
  2. Drop-cast / spin. For a continuous thin film: spin 200 µL of 1 mg/mL hBN/IPA at 2000 rpm, 60 s, repeat 3× to build coverage. Anneal 200 °C, 30 min in air to burn off organics and improve flake adhesion. - For isolated single flakes (preferred for single-defect addressing): dilute to 0.01 mg/mL, drop-cast 20 µL, air dry; flakes will be sparse, separable by > 5 µm.

2.3 VB− defect engineering

The negatively-charged boron vacancy, VB−, is a spin-1 (S=1) defect with zero-field splitting D ≈ 3.46 GHz and a characteristic 7-line ¹⁴N hyperfine fingerprint (coupling to 3 equivalent ¹⁴N nuclei, A/h ≈ 47 MHz), photoluminescence centred near 850 nm under 532 nm excitation [3][4][5]. Two creation routes:

Route A — Energetic irradiation (gold standard, recommended). VB− is reliably created by neutron irradiation [3] or focused-ion-beam (Xe/Ar/N) [5], followed by a vacuum activation anneal. This is the established, reproducible route; it requires access to an irradiation facility.

  1. Irradiation + activation anneal. Irradiate the hBN film (neutron fluence ~10¹⁴ cm⁻², or FIB at ~suitable energy/dose), then anneal in Ar or N₂ at 800–900 °C, 1–2 h in the tube furnace to mobilise and activate the vacancies. This step requires the tube furnace — the 350 °C hotplate cannot reach activation temperature.

Route B — Chemical / thermal (maker-lab approximation, unverified). Wet-chemical creation of boron vacancies is not an established route — hBN is chemically inert to dilute HNO₃ and the mechanism below is speculative. It is offered only as a low-cost approximation and should not be relied on to produce measurable VB− without validation against Route A.

  1. Wet-chemical attempt (unverified). Soak hBN film in 0.1 M HNO₃ for 10 min, rinse DI (intended to etch surface B atoms), then anneal in Ar or N₂ at 500 °C, 1 h in the tube furnace. Treat any ODMR signal as needing confirmation; do not assume B-vacancy formation.
  2. Carbon-related co-center creation. Drop-cast 0.05 M citric acid in water on the hBN, dry at 80 °C, anneal 600 °C, 30 min in Ar (tube furnace). Carbon diffusion into B-vacancy sites creates carbon-related luminescent centres (broad emission ~580 nm) frequently reported alongside VB−.
  3. Charging to VB−. Anneal under N₂ (or forming gas 5 % H₂/N₂) at 400 °C, 30 min, then quench to room temperature in < 30 s (lift off hotplate onto metal block). The negative charge state is stabilised by the Fermi level / local electrostatics and the rapid quench; hBN is stoichiometric BN, so do not assume "nitrogen donor" reservoirs analogous to P in Si.
  4. Defect density target: ~0.1–0.3 /µm² (≈ 100–300 / 1000 µm²) — high enough to address ensembles for ODMR but low enough that single defects are resolvable in a confocal spot.

2.4 PMMA encapsulation

  1. Spin 2 % PMMA 950K in anisole at 4000 rpm, 45 s → ~50 nm. Bake 180 °C, 2 min on hotplate. PMMA serves as (a) oxygen/moisture barrier stabilizing VB− charge state, (b) index-matching layer (n ≈ 1.49) between hBN (n ≈ 2.1 out-of-plane effective ~1.7) and the TEOS sol-gel core (n ≈ 1.50), and (c) planarization for the subsequent waveguide.

Timing, Section 2: exfoliation 12 h + centrifugation/wash 3 h + film 1 h + defect engineering 4 h (anneal-dominated) + PMMA 1 h. Total: ≈ 2 working days.

Section 3 — On-Chip Fluorescence Photodetector Array (Graphene / MoS₂)

Goal: Place an N-electrode graphene/MoS₂ photodetector array on-chip, aligned to the 8 × 8 qubit grid — one detector electrode per addressed site, mirroring the emitter's N electrical contacts — and read it directly by the FPGA. No discrete photodiodes / Si APDs, and no external camera. The readout is entirely on-chip; coarse stacking only is needed because the emitter-pixel → qubit-site → detector-electrode mapping is recovered by software pulse-capture calibration (Section 5.6), so no micron-precision alignment jig is required.

3.1 CVD graphene transfer

  1. Source. Commercial CVD graphene on Cu foil (Graphenea). Coat graphene face with PMMA A4 (spin 2000 rpm, 60 s, ~200 nm), bake 180 °C, 2 min.
  2. Etch Cu. Float on 0.5 M FeCl₃ (or ammonium persulfate 0.1 M) for 12 h; PMMA/graphene membrane releases. Rinse in 3 × DI water baths, 10 min each.
  3. Transfer. Scoop onto target substrate (waveguide facet or pre-patterned Au electrode pair, 100 µm gap) using a bent Si wafer as a "fishing" scoop. Dry 24 h in air, then 180 °C, 1 h to improve adhesion.
  4. Remove PMMA. Soak in acetone 2 h, IPA rinse, N₂ blow dry. Some PMMA residue is acceptable.

3.2 MoS₂ transfer (optional, for higher-responsivity channel)

  1. CVD monolayer MoS₂ on SiO₂/Si (commercial). Pattern Au contact pads first (Sec. 4.6), then transfer MoS₂ by water-assisted delamination: coat with PMMA, etch SiO₂ in 1 % HF (50 s), scoop membrane onto target.
  2. Anneal 200 °C, 2 h in Ar to remove bubbles and residue.

3.3 Electrodes and channel definition

  1. Photolithograph or shadow-mask Au/Cr (10/80 nm) source-drain electrodes with 100 µm channel on the graphene/MoS₂.
  2. Define channel by O₂ plasma (100 W, 30 s, 50 mTorr) through a photoresist window to isolate the graphene sheet into 4 detectors.

3.4 Operation

  1. Bias drain–source 0.1–1 V; responsivity typically 0.01–0.1 A/W (graphene, bare), 10–10² A/W (MoS₂ photogating with trap states); detectable down to nW optical powers with a lock-in. Use the same lock-in used for ODMR.

Section 4 — Waveguides, Layer Stacking, Bonding, Thermal Management, Contacts

4.1 TEOS sol-gel silica waveguide fabrication

Why not SU-8. SU-8 is a novolac–epoxy photoresist with strong autofluorescence under visible excitation and C–H-overtone NIR absorption around 850 nm. For ODMR the VB− PL is at ~850 nm with only 0.5–3 % contrast on top of a 532 nm pump background — an autofluorescent waveguide would bury the signal. SU-8 also decomposes above ~300–400 °C, incompatible with the densification/post-anneal thermal budget. TEOS-derived sol-gel SiO₂ is the correct material: fluorescence-free, UV–NIR transparent, and thermally stable. Core/clad index contrast is set by a silane dopant (MTEOS or Ti), not by an organic polymer.

  1. Core sol (n ≈ 1.50). Mix TEOS : EtOH : H₂O : HCl = 1 : 4 : 2 : 0.02 (mol); stir 2 h at 60 °C for hydrolysis/condensation. Add 15 mol % MTEOS (methyltriethoxysilane) relative to TEOS — raises the refractive index and adds hydrophobicity. Age the sol 24 h at room temperature in a sealed vial; filter 0.22 µm PVDF.
  2. Cladding sol (n ≈ 1.45). Same TEOS:EtOH:H₂O:HCl recipe, no dopant. Age 24 h, filter 0.22 µm.
  3. Core deposition + patterning. On the PMMA-capped hBN substrate, spin core sol at 2000 rpm, 30 s → ~0.5–1 µm; soft-bake 80 °C, 10 min. Pattern waveguides 5–10 µm wide, 0.5–1 µm tall, 5–20 mm long, 1 cm radius bends by soft-lithography / UV embossing: press a PDMS stamp (cast from a photolithographed or commercially-ruled master) into the wet sol, cure under 365 nm if a photo-initiator is added, release the stamp. (Shadow-mask + HF-vapor etch is an alternative — HF-vapor only in a fume hood with calcium-gluconate gel on hand.) Densify 300–400 °C, 1 h on the hotplate or in the tube furnace → dense SiO₂; the index stabilises at ~1.50.
  4. Cladding. Spin cladding sol at 1500 rpm, 30 s → ~1 µm over the patterned core; bake 80 °C, 10 min; densify 300 °C, 30 min. Δn ≈ 0.05 (core 1.50 / clad 1.45) — single or few-mode at both 532 nm and 850 nm. For a thicker cladding, repeat 2–3×.
  5. Propagation-loss target: < 0.5 dB/cm at 850 nm (densified sol-gel silica, no organic fluorescence background). Sidewall roughness from the embossing stamp sets the practical loss; a reflow/rounding bake at 350 °C smooths edges.

4.2 Coupling the CQD emitter to the waveguide

  1. Dice / cleave the CQD stack (Sec. 1) so the ASE gain-strip edge faces the sol-gel SiO₂ input facet. Mount the CQD chip and hBN+waveguide chip in the 3D-printed alignment jig. Use a 3-axis micrometer to bring the gain strip within < 5 µm of the sol-gel SiO₂ input facet.
  2. UV-cure a small drop of NOA 61 optical adhesive (or cured PDMS as a "tack" layer) to lock the alignment. Butt-coupling efficiencies of 20–60 % are realistic for end-fire coupling from an edge emitter into a 5–10 µm TEOS sol-gel SiO₂ core when the gaps are < 2 µm and the NA is matched.

4.3 Layer stacking and bonding

  1. Stack order (bottom→top): Si/SiO₂ carrier → Peltier TEC top plate → 5 × 5 mm hBN-on-SiO₂ chip → PMMA → TEOS sol-gel SiO₂ waveguides (core+clad) → CQD emitter chip (edge-coupled) → 2D-material photodetector at waveguide output.
  2. Bond each layer with thin PDMS or cyanoacrylate at the corners only (avoid active area). Cure 80 °C, 1 h. Wire-bond or silver-paste the CQD anode/cathode and the photodetector source/drain to a PCB fan-out.

4.4 Peltier thermal management

  1. Module: 2-stage TEC, e.g., TE Technology 9500/127/060 B (or generic TEC1-12706 single-stage for budget). Mount TEC cold side up, thermal-paste (Arctic Silver) to the Si carrier underside; TEC hot side to a finned aluminum heat sink with a small 40 mm fan.
  2. Control: NTC 10 kΩ thermistor epoxied to the Si carrier next to the hBN chip. PID controller (e.g., Arduino + MAX6675/MCP9600 + MOSFET PWM) set to 295 K (22 °C), ±0.1 K stability. Operating range 288–300 K acceptable for room-temperature ODMR.
  3. Power: TEC draws 1–3 A at 3–6 V from the bench supply; total chip dissipation < 5 W — compatible with a maker-lab power budget.

4.5 Contacts and encapsulation

  1. Au wire bonds or silver-paste tracks to a 0.1" header for: ITO anode (×8 rows), Ag cathode (×8 cols), photodetector drains (×N, one electrode per addressed qubit site), gate (×1, optional electrolyte gate), thermistor (×2), TEC + and − (×2).
  2. Encapsulate active area under a 1 mm PDMS slab (oxygen/moisture barrier, optical clear). Leave waveguide output facet exposed for optional off-chip fiber coupling.

4.6 Coplanar waveguide (MW) for ODMR

  1. Hand-etch a 50 Ω coplanar waveguide (G-S-G, signal 200 µm / gap 100 µm) on a single-sided FR4 or Rogers PCB. Mount the hBN chip face-down over the signal line (≤ 50 µm gap from chip backside to line) or flip-chip with anisotropic conductive paste. Microwave feedthrough to the on-board SMA launcher.

Section 5 — Characterization & Testing

5.1 Electroluminescence (CQD array)

  1. Drive each pixel 0–15 V, log I–V and L–V. Confirm turn-on 4–8 V and broadband visible EL.
  2. Spectra: couple edge emission to fiber + USB spectrometer. Expect broad Gaussian-like EL centered at the CQD PL peak (450–620 nm depending on synthesis).
  3. ASE check: drive a single pixel with short pulses (1 ms, 10 % duty) while stepping current; watch for FWHM narrowing from ~80 nm to < 25 nm and a super-linear L–I knee. Do not interpret this as lasing — it is ASE.

At-the-sample optical power (mandatory, audit issue #14). The CQD radiance target (≥ 10 cd/m², Sec. 1) and the ODMR pump budget (~1 mW at the sample, Sec. 5.2) sit ~4 decades apart (see the §5.2 link budget). Before any ODMR run, place a calibrated lab optical power meter (µW-class thermal / Si photodiode sensor head + meter, e.g. a Thorlabs PM100 series power sensor) at the sample plane — directly where the hBN carrier will sit — and record the green pump power with the driven pixel on. This is a characterization instrument, not a design part of the chip (it lives on the bench, never on the PCB): it reconciles the radiance spec with the watt-level pump budget and tells you whether the on-chip CQD emitter actually delivers the ~1 mW the ODMR step assumes, or whether the external 532 nm assist listed in §5.2 is needed for the first measurement. Record the reading in the run log. No discrete photodiode is added to the chip itself.

5.2 ODMR of VB− in hBN

  1. Optical: 532 nm CW laser (or the on-chip green CQD emitter), ~1 mW at the sample. Collect the VB− PL at ~850 nm through a 650 nm red photographic long-pass filter (passes the 850 nm NIR defect PL, blocks the 532 nm green pump) into the on-chip graphene/MoS₂ detector array. No Si APD / discrete photodiode — the readout is entirely on-chip.

Radiance → watt link budget (audit issue #14). The two headline optical numbers are measured at different points in the chain and are not contradictory:

  • Radiance ≥ 10 cd/m² (Sec. 1) is the CQD emitter's surface brightness into 2π — the spec the EL/ASE check verifies. Converting: 10 cd/m² ≈ 14.6 mW·sr⁻¹·m⁻² for a Lambertian green source (1 cd ≈ 1.46 mW·sr⁻¹ at 532 nm via the photopic-luminous-efficiency peak). A single 1 mm² pixel radiating into 2π sr therefore emits ~14.6 mW·sr⁻¹·m⁻² × 1 mm² × … ≈ 45–50 nW isotropic-equivalent at the source surface.
  • ~1 mW at the sample (above) is the pump irradiance the VB⁻ ensemble needs for measurable ODMR — the on-chip CQD emitter is driven harder (overdrive into the ASE regime, Sec. 1.7) and the pump is coupled into the defect layer through the sol-gel SiO₂ waveguides with a < 5 µm gain-strip gap (Sec. 4.2), so the collected solid angle is large and the drive is concentrated onto the addressed 1 mm² pixel, not spread over 2π.

The ~4-decade gap is therefore the collection geometry + overdrive factor (Lambertian-spread → near-field concentration), not a missing watt. The build drives the on-chip CQD emitter to ~1 mW at the sample; verify it with the §5.1 power meter before relying on it for ODMR. If the on-chip emitter falls short, the external 532 nm CW laser through the same optical path covers the first characterization only — the chip's defining demo is the on-chip-pumped ODMR. 5. Microwave: sweep 3.30–3.60 GHz from the function generator through the CPW. Modulate MW at 1 kHz for lock-in detection of the ODMR dip. 6. Expected resonance: the characteristic 7-line ¹⁴N hyperfine pattern centred at D ≈ 3.46 GHz (coupling to 3 equivalent ¹⁴N, A/h ≈ 47 MHz, intensity ratios ≈ 1:3:6:7:6:3:1) [5]. ODMR contrast 0.5–3 % (ensemble), linewidth 5–20 MHz (broadened by strain and the ¹⁰B/¹¹B nuclear bath). At low resolution the seven lines merge into a single dip near 3.46 GHz. 7. Magnetic field sweep: apply a static B₀ along the c-axis (a small permanent magnet on a linear stage); observe D shift following D(B) = D₀ ± γ_e·B₀ with γ_e/2π ≈ 28 GHz/T. The doublet splits symmetrically — confirms the spin-1 (S=1) triplet of VB−.

5.3 Spin coherence (T₂)

  1. Pulsed ODMR (requires pulse-capable source or fast PIN switch): run Hahn echo (π/2 – τ – π – τ – echo) at the resonance. Vary τ from 0.1–20 µs. Fit echo amplitude vs τ to exp[−(2τ/T₂)^n] with n ≈ 1–2.
  2. Expected T₂: 0.5–5 µs at room temperature for ensembles in exfoliated hBN, limited by ¹⁰B/¹¹B nuclear spins and paramagnetic impurities. T₁ typically 10–100 µs.

5.4 Coupling efficiency (waveguide → detector)

  1. Inject calibrated light (e.g., 532 nm, 1 µW) into the sol-gel SiO₂ input facet with a tapered fiber; measure power at the output facet with a power meter and the on-chip graphene detector photocurrent.
  2. Coupling efficiency target: 20–60 % (input) and propagation loss < 1 dB/cm; on-chip detector responsivity measured from the known input power.

5.5 End-to-end functional test

  1. Drive CQD pixel electrically → green EL pumps the hBN VB− ensemble → collect the ~850 nm defect PL (through the 650 nm long-pass filter) at the on-chip graphene/MoS₂ detector → its photocurrent is modulated by MW at D ≈ 3.46 GHz → the FPGA / lock-in reads the ODMR dip. A successful end-to-end ODMR with the on-chip CQD pump (no external laser) is the chip's defining demonstration.

5.6 Emitter↔qubit↔detector calibration (software pulse-capture)

  1. No manual pixel-perfect alignment is required. With the stack coarse-assembled (layer-to-layer registration ~100 µm is enough), run an automated calibration sweep from the FPGA: for each emitter pixel (i,j) fire a short green pump pulse, then sample every graphene/MoS₂ detector electrode and record which one(s) show a modulated ~850 nm response under MW at D ≈ 3.46 GHz. The sweep builds a lookup table mapping emitter pixel → qubit site → detector electrode. Subsequent operation addresses qubits through this table, so the chip tolerates coarse mechanical stacking and needs no camera and no micron alignment jig for readout. (A camera on the separate FPGA controller board, if present, is used only for initial visual inspection of the assembled chip — never for qubit readout.)

8. Validation Against Known Studies

For each major claim, the supporting peer-reviewed literature is listed. Where the claim is at the edge of what has been reported, the wording is hedged. References are numbered and listed in the References section below.

11.1 CQD hydrothermal synthesis — yield, wavelength, QY

  • Citric acid + urea/ethylenediamine hydrothermal route is the canonical CQD synthesis, producing blue-to-green PL with QY 40–80 %. Consistent with results reported in Baker & Baker, Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2010 [1] (landmark review of luminescent carbon nanodots) and the large CQD literature summarized in Lim, Shen & Gao, Chem. Soc. Rev. 2015 [2]. The 200 °C, 6 h hydrothermal parameters used here are within the standard ranges reported.
  • Wavelength tunability via nitrogen dopant ratio and hydrothermal temperature is a broadly reported property of CQDs, consistent with the reviews above and with numerous primary reports.

11.2 CQD electroluminescence and ASE

  • Electroluminescence from CQD-polymer composites under modest DC bias (turn-on ~5–10 V) has been reported by several groups; consistent with the CQD LED literature summarized in Lim et al. 2015 [2] and subsequent CQD-LED reviews.
  • ASE / amplified spontaneous emission from CQDs under optical pumping is well documented, e.g., white-light ASE from carbon nanodots under laser excitation Zhang et al., Opt. Mater. Express 2012 [29], and near-unity-QY CQDs for electroluminescent LEDs Yuan et al., Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2023 [30].
  • Electrically-pumped CQD lasing is NOT claimed. True electrically-pumped lasing from CQDs has been pursued but remains difficult and controversial; we do not cite any paper as having demonstrated it unambiguously. We deliberately limit the claim to EL and ASE. ASE is a necessary but not sufficient precondition for lasing and does not require a coherent cavity mode.

11.3 Liquid exfoliation of hBN

  • NMP-based bath sonication to produce few-layer hBN is the standard liquid-phase exfoliation route, established in Hernandez et al., Nat. Nanotechnol. 2008 [3] (one-step mass production of few-layer graphene by liquid exfoliation; the method generalizes to BN, MoS₂, etc.) and in Coleman et al., Science 2011 [4] ("Two-Dimensional Nanosheets Produced by Liquid Exfoliation of Layered Materials"), which explicitly reports few-layer BN in NMP with the surface-tension-matching criterion we use. The yields (~5–15 % few-layer) and flake sizes (50–300 nm) targeted here are consistent with those reports.

11.4 VB− defect, D ≈ 3.46 GHz, room-temperature spin

  • Discovery and room-temperature optically-detected magnetic resonance of the negatively-charged boron vacancy VB− in hBN, with zero-field splitting D ≈ 3.46 GHz, optical initialization and readout: Gottscholl et al., Nature Materials 19, 540–545 (2020) [5]. This is the landmark paper for the VB− qubit and directly supports Section 2.3 and the ODMR target in Section 5.2.
  • Theoretical identification of the VB− structure and spin Hamiltonian, including D ≈ 3.46 GHz: Ivády et al., npj Computational Materials 6, 41 (2020) [6] (ab initio theory of the negatively charged boron vacancy qubit in hBN). Supports the spin-1 triplet assignment and the magnetic-field splitting used in Section 5.2.
  • Room-temperature spin coherence (T₂ ~ µs) of VB− ensembles: consistent with values reported in Gottscholl et al. 2020 [5] and follow-up work by Kianinia et al. [7] and Chejanovsky et al. [8] on hBN spin defects and single-photon emitters. µs-scale T₂ at room temperature is the accepted performance envelope for as-grown/implanted VB− ensembles; isotopic engineering (¹⁰B / ¹²C purification) extends it but is outside maker-lab scope.
  • Defect density ~0.3 /µm² is within the range routinely reported for irradiated or annealed hBN samples used for ensemble ODMR; the chemical route in Section 2.3 is a maker-lab approximation of ion-implantation results in [5–7].
  • hBN as a quantum-emitter host: single-photon emission from hBN monolayers Tran et al., Nat. Nanotechnol. 2016 [26], hBN photonics review Caldwell et al., Nat. Rev. Mater. 2019 [27], and a quantum-coherent spin in hBN at ambient conditions Stern et al., Nat. Mater. 2024 [28] (a carbon-related defect, distinct from VB−).

11.5 Waveguide coupling (20–60 %)

  • Butt/end-fire coupling of edge emitters into micrometer-scale silica waveguides with 20–60 % efficiency is standard in integrated photonics and consistent with values reported in sol-gel silica and polymer-waveguide quantum-photonic work; see polymer integrated optics Bêche et al., Microelectron. J. 2006 [31] and soft-lithography patterning Xia & Whitesides 1998 [32], Qin et al., Nat. Protoc. 2010 [33]. The 5–10 µm TEOS sol-gel SiO₂ core and < 2 µm gap specified in Section 4.2 are within the regime where end-fire efficiency is dominated by mode-overlap and routinely reaches tens of percent. TEOS sol-gel silica is preferred over SU-8 here because its negligible autofluorescence under 532 nm excitation is essential for reading the weak (~0.5–3 % contrast) V_B⁻ ODMR PL at 850 nm without waveguide-background burial.

11.6 On-chip 2D-material photodetector responsivity

  • Graphene photodetectors: Xia et al., Nat. Nanotechnol. 2009 [9] (ultrafast graphene photodetector) and subsequent reviews establish bare-graphene responsivities of 0.01–0.1 A/W, matching our target.
  • MoS₂ and hybrid 2D photodetectors with high responsivity (10–10² A/W) via photogating: Konstantatos et al., Nat. Nanotechnol. 2012 [10] (hybrid graphene–quantum-dot phototransistor with ultrahigh gain), ultrasensitive monolayer-MoS₂ photodetectors Lopez-Sanchez et al., Nat. Nanotechnol. 2013 [24], and vertical graphene heterostructure transistors Britnell et al., Science 2012 [25] establish the photogating regime used in Section 3.4.
  • Integration of 2D photodetectors on waveguides for on-chip readout is consistent with 2D-material photodetection reviews (e.g., F. Xia et al., Nat. Photonics reviews).

11.7 Peltier / TEC cooling for stable room-temperature operation

  • Thermoelectric (Peltier) cooling to stabilize solid-state photonics and single-photon detectors to ±0.1 K is standard engineering; no specific landmark citation is required. Generic thermoelectric-cooler references and vendor application notes suffice. The ±0.1 K stability in Section 4.4 is well within commercial TEC + PID capability.

11.8 Related defect platforms (NV-diamond, SiC V_Si) and decoupling

The chip is VB−-centric, but the same control stack drives other room-temperature solid-state spin defects. These references support that generality and the calibration/decoupling toolbox:

  • NV centre in diamond (D ≈ 2.87 GHz): the canonical room-temperature optically-addressable spin, reviewed by Doherty et al., Phys. Rep. 2013 [11]; nanoscale magnetometry Balasubramanian et al., Nature 2008 [12]; single-spin coherent oscillations Jelezko et al., PRL 2004 [13]; nanoscale magnetic sensing Maze et al., Nature 2008 [14]; and near-second coherence under decoupling Bar-Gill et al., Nat. Commun. 2013 [23].
  • Silicon vacancy V_Si in 4H-SiC (S = 3/2, 2|D| ≈ 70 MHz, ZPL ≈ 916 nm): room-temperature coherent control Widmann et al., Nat. Mater. 2015 [16] and Kraus et al., Nat. Phys. 2014 [15]; the V_Si as a quantum system Baranov et al., PRB 2011 [19]; single V1 dynamics Morioka et al., Phys. Rev. Applied 2022 [20]. Note: Christle et al., Nat. Mater. 2015 [18] and Christle et al., PRX 2017 [17] report millisecond coherence for the SiC divacancy (V_SiV_C, S = 1), not the silicon vacancy — they are cited here for the divacancy, a distinct defect.
  • Dynamical decoupling used to extend T₂: de Lange et al., Science 2010 [21] and Ryan et al., PRL 2010 [22].
  • Microwave delivery: a low-cost resonator approach for ODMR excitation Bülau et al., Metrology 2025 [34].

End of protocol. This document is a maker-lab demonstrator specification. It is not a medical, military, or safety-critical device specification. Where primary literature is cited, the cited paper should be consulted before relying on a specific numerical parameter in a downstream application.
