Quantum Solid State Chip (Room Temperature) — Fabrication Protocol · NV-Diamond variant

Project: QSSC (Quantum Solid State Chip, room-temperature demonstrator) Document type: Laboratory fabrication protocol (standalone variant) Version: 1.0 Variant: NV-diamond (negatively-charged nitrogen-vacancy, NV⁻) Scope: Same chip architecture as the hBN base protocol — (1) an electrically-pumped carbon-quantum-dot (CQD) green ASE/LED emitter array and (2) an on-chip 2D-material (graphene/MoS₂) photodetector array with FPGA readout and software pulse-capture calibration — but the middle spin-host block is a CVD diamond plate hosting NV⁻ centres (zero-field splitting D ≈ 2.87 GHz, PL 637–800 nm, T₂ ≈ 1.8 ms at 300 K). The optical/electronic chain (green CQD pump, 650 nm red long-pass filter, graphene detector, ADF4351 MW) is unchanged from the hBN chip; only the host, the defect-creation step, the geometry (bulk vs 2D) and the MW frequency change.

Microdiamond-layer option (home-lab / budget). Instead of a bulk CVD plate, the spin host can be a layer of commercial NV⁻ microdiamonds (~100 nm, pre-activated, $30–60/100 mg) dispersed in a TEOS sol-gel SiO₂ matrix and spin-coated on a 10×10 mm glass carrier. This trades the bulk plate's ~ms T₂ for µs-scale ensemble T₂ (surface-proximity-limited) but removes the tube-furnace and the $50–250 plate, making it the route used by the home-lab prototype.

Note. The NV⁻ variant is the easiest platform swap in the QSSC family because the NV⁻ is pumped at 532 nm green — exactly the colour the on-chip CQD emitter already makes — and its red/NIR PL (637–800 nm) passes the same 650 nm long-pass filter and is read by the same graphene/MoS₂ array as the hBN chip. The emitter, filter, detector and FPGA are reused verbatim. What you give up relative to hBN is the 2D / van-der-Waals integrability and the atomically-flat surface proximity: diamond is a bulk 3D plate, so the emitter and detector sit on top of the diamond rather than in a stacked 2D heterostructure. What you gain is ~900× better room-temperature coherence (T₂ 1.8 ms vs 2 µs), enough to run small quantum algorithms at 300 K with dynamical decoupling.

On-chip emitter (no big diodes). This variant keeps the on-chip CQD emitter as the pump — no external laser diode is required. (The SiC variant is the one that needs a different on-chip emitter; diamond reuses the green CQD unchanged.)

Two diamond routes, one canonical die (audit issue #15). The QSSC chip is built around a single canonical 20 × 20 mm two-faced die (130 pads, 2.5 mm pitch, 64 electrodes + 1 common per face — see hardware/HARDWARE_DESIGN.md). The diamond host can be realized two ways, both centred on that die: 1. Bulk ¹²C-enriched CVD diamond — the compute route described in this protocol (T₂ ≈ 1.8 ms at 300 K, enough to run small Shor-15). This is the route the NV simulator profile's 300 K compute claim is scoped to. 2. Spin-coated NV⁻ microdiamond in TEOS sol-gel — the sensor-array route (the microdiamond-layer option above): ~100 nm pre-activated microdiamonds give µs–tens-of-µs ensemble T₂ (surface-proximity-limited), suitable for sensing, not for running quantum algorithms. Do not read the 1.8 ms / 300 K-compute figure as applying to the as-built microdiamond sensor array — it is a bulk-CVD compute-material figure.

This protocol documents the bulk-CVD compute route; a full microdiamond sensor-array protocol rewrite is flagged as a follow-up (the P0 prototype covers the home-lab version today).

1. Abstract

This document specifies a complete, equipment-minimal fabrication protocol for a room-temperature solid-state spin-defect quantum chip (QSSC) built on a CVD diamond plate hosting NV⁻ centres. The chip combines three functional blocks on a single 25 × 25 mm² carrier: (i) a hydrothermally-synthesized green (~520–540 nm) carbon quantum dot (CQD) emitter array in a polymeric LED/ASE stack (ITO/PEDOT:PSS/CQD–PVA/ZnO/Ag) that doubles as the optical pump for the NV⁻ (532 nm excitation band); (ii) a commercial CVD diamond plate with NV⁻ centres (D ≈ 2.87 GHz, ZPL 637 nm, phonon sideband to ~800 nm, T₂ ≈ 1.8 ms at 300 K), cleaned and (if needed) annealed to activate implanted vacancies; and (iii) an on-chip graphene/MoS₂ photodetector array that reads the red/NIR NV PL directly, with software pulse-capture calibration. The die sits on a passive heat-spreader at 288–300 K. All processing uses benchtop chemistry, a spin coater, a hotplate, contact-mask UV, a tube furnace (for the optional NV-activation anneal) and a ~USD 3,000–3,500 equipment footprint beyond an optical microscope.

2. Introduction & Scientific Basis

2.1 Why NV-diamond for the same chip

The negatively-charged nitrogen-vacancy centre (NV⁻) in diamond is the canonical room-temperature optically-addressable spin: spin-1 (S=1), zero-field splitting D ≈ 2.87 GHz, optical initialization and single-shot readout at 300 K, and Hahn-echo coherence T₂ ≈ 1.8 ms in ¹²C-enriched diamond (≈0.5–0.7 ms in natural-abundance diamond) — extended toward 1 s with dynamical decoupling. For the QSSC architecture this means the same control/readout stack as the hBN chip drives a qubit that can actually run small quantum algorithms at room temperature, not only sense.

2.2 Why the green CQD emitter is reused unchanged

NV⁻ is optically excited at 532 nm — the same green band the citric-acid+urea CQDs already produce at ~220 °C hydrothermal temperature. So the entire Section-1 emitter (ITO/PEDOT:PSS/CQD–PVA/ZnO/Ag, green, silver-paste cathode) is reused without modification. The on-chip emitter pumps the NV⁻ directly; no external 532 nm laser is needed for the end-to-end demo.

2.3 Why the detector and filter are reused unchanged

NV⁻ photoluminescence is the zero-phonon line at 637 nm plus a broad phonon sideband extending to ~800 nm (red/NIR). The 650 nm red photographic long-pass passes the 637–800 nm PL and blocks the 532 nm green pump — the same filter used for the hBN chip. The graphene/MoS₂ photodetector reads 637–800 nm; MoS₂ (monolayer bandgap ~690 nm) actually absorbs a larger share of the NV PL than it does of the hBN 850 nm emission, so the detector works at least as well here.

Section 1 — CQD Emitter Array (UNCHANGED from hBN base)

Goal: Synthesize green (~520–540 nm) N-doped CQDs and integrate them into a patterned ITO/PEDOT:PSS/CQD–PVA/ZnO/Ag electroluminescent diode array (8 × 8 pixels, 1 mm² each) on a 25 × 25 mm² ITO-glass substrate. The green emitter doubles as the pump for the NV⁻ (532 nm band). This section is identical to the hBN protocol Section 1 — the same citric-acid+urea hydrothermal route at ~220 °C, the same p-i-n stack, the same ZnO ETL and silver-paste cathode. No ethylenediamine is required; emission colour is set by hydrothermal temperature (~220 °C → green).

Key parameters (unchanged): CQD PL peak 520–540 nm (green), EL turn-on 4–8 V, ASE detectable under overdrive (FWHM narrowing < 25 nm), target radiance ≥ 10 cd/m². The full step-by-step procedure (1.1 hydrothermal synthesis, 1.2 CQD–PVA ink, 1.3 ITO substrate, 1.4 PEDOT:PSS, 1.5 CQD emissive layer, 1.5b ZnO ETL, 1.6 silver-paste cathode, 1.7 EL/ASE check) is reproduced verbatim from the hBN base protocol — see FABRICATION_PROTOCOL.md Section 1. Only the destination of the pump light changes (NV⁻ in diamond instead of V_B⁻ in hBN).

Timing: ≈ 3 working days (synthesis 8 h + dialysis 48 h unattended + film stack 1 day).

Section 2 — Diamond Plate Preparation + NV⁻ (REPLACES the hBN section)

Goal: Obtain a clean CVD diamond plate hosting optically-addressable NV⁻ centres (D ≈ 2.87 GHz) at ensemble density suitable for 8 × 8 site addressing, with the surface ready to receive the emitter and detector on top.

2.1 Procurement

  1. Buy the plate. Order a CVD diamond plate with NV⁻ centres (~2×2×0.5 mm to ~3×3×0.5 mm, optical/CVD grade, NV density ~10¹⁰–10¹² cm⁻³). Search terms: "CVD diamond NV centre", "nitrogen vacancy diamond plate", "Type Ib diamond". For a budget demonstration, a small Type Ib HPHT plate (natural substitutional nitrogen, which forms NV⁻ on anneal) is adequate. Expect ~$50–$250 retail.
  2. Spec check. Confirm with the vendor that NV⁻ are present and optically active (vendor ODMR plot if available). If buying bare CVD N-doped diamond without NV⁻ formed, you must implant/anneal (Route B below) — prefer buying NV-ready to avoid an ion implanter.

2.2 Surface cleaning

  1. Solvent clean. Sonicate 5 min each in acetone, IPA, DI water.
  2. Acid clean. Boil in H₂SO₄:HNO₃:H₂O₂ = 3:1:1 at ~90 °C for 30 min (removes graphitic/surface contamination, leaves an oxygen-terminated surface). Cool, then rinse copiously in DI water. (Fume hood, full PPE — strong oxidizer.) - Alternative: O₂ plasma (100 W, 10 min) for a dry, mask-friendly clean if you want to avoid hot acid.
  3. Final rinse. 0.1 M HNO₃ dip 1 min, DI rinse, N₂ blow dry. Surface is now O-terminated, hydrophilic, ready for layer deposition.

2.3 NV⁻ activation (ONLY if the plate needs it)

Route A — Pre-activated (preferred, no furnace needed). Many CVD-NV plates arrive with NV⁻ already formed. If the vendor confirms optically-active NV⁻, skip to 2.4. No high-temperature step is required.

Route B — Anneal to form NV⁻ (if vacancies are present but not yet bound to N, e.g. as-implanted or irradiated plate).

  1. Vacuum / Ar anneal. Place the cleaned plate in the tube furnace, pump to < 10⁻⁵ mbar (or flowing Ar / forming gas 5 % H₂). Ramp to 800 °C, 2 h. This mobilizes vacancies, which diffuse to substitutional N and form NV⁻. Cool to < 100 °C in Ar before removing. This step needs the tube furnace — the 350 °C hotplate cannot reach it.
  2. Optional surface graphitization removal. Re-do step 4 (boiling acid) for 10 min to remove any surface graphitization from the anneal.

Note on creating NV⁻ from scratch (not recommended for the maker route): Growing NV⁻ requires either CVD growth with in-situ N doping (needs a CVD diamond reactor) or N⁺ ion implantation + 800–1200 °C anneal (needs an ion implanter). Both are outside maker scope. Buy the plate with NV⁻ already in it — this is the whole point of choosing diamond for this variant.

2.4 Site density and surface proximity

  1. Density. Ensemble NV⁻ density ~10¹⁰–10¹² cm⁻³ is typical for CVD-NV plates; at the plate surface this gives resolvable ensemble spots for 8 × 8 ODMR. If single-site addressing is desired, buy a low-density (~10⁹ cm⁻³) plate.
  2. Depth caveat. Unlike 2D hBN where defects sit at the surface, NV⁻ in a bulk plate are distributed through the ~0.5 mm thickness. For sensing you may want shallow NV⁻ (within ~10 nm of the surface); specify "shallow NV" or "delta-doped" with the vendor. For the QSSC compute/sensor demonstrator, ensemble bulk NV⁻ is fine; the trade-off vs hBN is worse surface proximity, far better coherence.

2.5 Host surface condition

  1. Diamond is chemically inert and the NV⁻ charge state is intrinsically stable at the surface (unlike V_B⁻), so the cleaned surface goes straight to emitter and detector deposition (Section 4). Spin a thin TEOS sol-gel SiO₂ planarization layer first if the surface is too rough for adhesion.

Timing, Section 2: clean 1 h + (optional) anneal 4 h. Total: ~0.5–1 working day (far faster than hBN exfoliation + defect engineering).

Section 3 — On-Chip Fluorescence Photodetector Array (Graphene / MoS₂) (UNCHANGED)

Goal: N-electrode graphene/MoS₂ photodetector array on-chip, read directly by the FPGA, software pulse-capture calibration (Section 5.6). Identical to the hBN protocol Section 3. The only platform note: NV⁻ PL (637–800 nm) is better matched to MoS₂ (monolayer bandgap ~690 nm) than hBN's 850 nm emission, so the MoS₂ photogating channel contributes more gain here; bare graphene still carries the broadband absorption up to ~800 nm.

  • CVD graphene transfer, MoS₂ transfer, Au/Cr source-drain electrodes, O₂-plasma channel isolation, bias 0.1–1 V, lock-in readout — all as in the hBN base protocol. No discrete photodiodes, no external camera.

Section 4 — Stacking (bulk geometry), Thermal Path, Contacts

4.1 Stacking (bulk geometry)

  1. Stack order (bottom→top): Si/SiO₂ carrier → diamond plate (face up) → CQD emitter chip (green face toward the diamond, edge-coupled) → graphene/MoS₂ photodetector over the diamond's PL side. Bond the corners with a thin cyanoacrylate layer; cure 80 °C, 1 h. Wire-bond or silver-paste the CQD anode/cathode and the detector source/drain to a PCB fan-out.

4.2 Thermal path

  1. Cu/Al heat-spreader clamped under the carrier. Diamond's thermal conductivity (~2000 W/m·K, 4× better than Si) holds the die at ambient on its own, so the chip runs uncooled.

4.3 Contacts and encapsulation

  1. Au wire / silver-paste to a 0.1" header: ITO anode (×8 rows), Ag cathode (×8 cols), detector drains (×N), gate (×1). Encapsulate under a 1 mm PDMS slab, leaving the detector aperture open.

4.4 Coplanar waveguide (MW) for ODMR (frequency changes)

  1. Hand-etch a 50 Ω CPW on FR4/Rogers; mount the diamond plate face-down over the signal line (≤ 50 µm gap) or flip-chip with anisotropic conductive paste. Microwave frequency changes from 3.46 GHz (hBN) to 2.87 GHz (NV⁻) — well within the ADF4351 range (35 MHz–4.4 GHz), no hardware change.

Section 5 — Characterization & Testing

5.1 Electroluminescence (CQD array) — unchanged

Drive each pixel 0–15 V, confirm turn-on 4–8 V and broadband green EL; ASE check (FWHM narrowing < 25 nm). As in the hBN protocol.

At-the-sample optical power (mandatory, audit issue #14). The CQD radiance target (≥ 10 cd/m², Sec. 1) and the ODMR pump budget (~1 mW at the sample, Sec. 5.2) sit ~4 decades apart (see the §5.2 link budget). Before any ODMR run, place a calibrated lab optical power meter (µW-class thermal / Si photodiode sensor head + meter, e.g. a Thorlabs PM100 series power sensor) at the sample plane — directly where the diamond will sit — and record the green pump power with the driven pixel on. This is a characterization instrument, not a design part of the chip (it lives on the bench, never on the PCB): it reconciles the radiance spec with the watt-level pump budget and tells you whether the on-chip CQD emitter actually delivers the ~1 mW the ODMR step assumes, or whether an external 532 nm assist is needed for the first measurement. Record the reading in the run log. No discrete photodiode is added to the chip itself.

5.2 ODMR of NV⁻ in diamond

  1. Optical: on-chip green CQD emitter (~532 nm, ~1 mW at the sample) — no external laser. Collect NV⁻ PL at 637–800 nm through the 650 nm red photographic long-pass filter (passes the red/NIR NV PL, blocks the 532 nm green pump) into the on-chip graphene/MoS₂ detector array. No Si APD / discrete photodiode.

Radiance → watt link budget (audit issue #14). The two headline optical numbers are measured at different points in the chain and are not contradictory:

  • Radiance ≥ 10 cd/m² (Sec. 1) is the CQD emitter's surface brightness into 2π — the spec the EL/ASE check verifies. Converting: 10 cd/m² ≈ 14.6 mW·sr⁻¹·m⁻² for a Lambertian green source (1 cd ≈ 1.46 mW·sr⁻¹ at 532 nm via the photopic-luminous-efficiency peak). A single 1 mm² pixel radiating into 2π sr therefore emits ~14.6 mW·sr⁻¹·m⁻² × 1 mm² × … ≈ 45–50 nW isotropic-equivalent at the source surface.
  • ~1 mW at the sample (above) is the pump irradiance the NV⁻ ensemble needs for measurable ODMR — the on-chip CQD emitter is driven harder (overdrive into the ASE regime, Sec. 1.7) and the diamond is held within ~tens of µm of the emitter, so the collected solid angle is large and the drive is concentrated onto the ~3×3 mm plate, not spread over 2π.

The ~4-decade gap is therefore the collection geometry + overdrive factor (Lambertian-spread → near-field concentration), not a missing watt. The build drives the on-chip CQD emitter to ~1 mW at the sample; verify it with the §5.1 power meter before relying on it for ODMR. If the on-chip emitter falls short, an external 532 nm laser diode through the same optical path is the fallback for the first characterization only — the chip's defining demo is the on-chip-pumped ODMR. 5. Microwave: sweep 2.80–2.94 GHz from the function generator / ADF4351 through the CPW. Modulate MW at 1 kHz for lock-in detection of the ODMR dip. 6. Expected resonance: a single dip at D ≈ 2.87 GHz (the ¹⁴N 3-line hyperfine, A ≈ 2.16 MHz, is sub-linewidth and usually merges into one dip at B=0; it splits into a doublet under a static B₀). ODMR contrast 2–30 % (ensemble/single), linewidth 1–10 MHz. NV⁻ ODMR contrast is higher than V_B⁻ (0.5–3 %), so readout is easier. 7. Magnetic field sweep: apply B₀ along the NV axis; observe D shift D(B) = D₀ ± γ_e·B₀, γ_e/2π ≈ 28 GHz/T; the resonance splits into a doublet — confirms the S=1 triplet.

5.3 Spin coherence (T₂)

  1. Pulsed ODMR (Hahn echo, π/2–τ–π–τ–echo). Vary τ from 1 µs to 2 ms. Fit echo vs τ to exp[−(2τ/T₂)^n].
  2. Expected T₂: 0.5–1.8 ms at room temperature (≈0.5–0.7 ms natural-abundance, ≈1.8 ms in ¹²C-enriched CVD). T₁ typically 3–7 ms. This is ~900× longer than V_B⁻ and is why this variant can run small quantum algorithms at 300 K. With dynamical decoupling (XY8/CPMG) T₂ extends further.

5.4 Emitter → detector coupling

Inject calibrated 532 nm light at the emitter edge and measure the on-chip detector photocurrent. Target 20–60 % of the emitted pump reaching the detector.

5.5 End-to-end functional test

  1. Drive the CQD pixel electrically → green EL pumps the NV⁻ ensemble → collect 637–800 nm NV PL (through the 650 nm long-pass filter) at the on-chip graphene/MoS₂ detector → photocurrent modulated by MW at 2.87 GHz → FPGA/lock-in reads the ODMR dip. Successful end-to-end ODMR with the on-chip CQD pump (no external laser) is the chip's defining demonstration — identical concept to the hBN chip, at a different MW frequency and with ~900× more coherence headroom.

5.6 Emitter↔qubit↔detector calibration (software pulse-capture) — unchanged

  1. Same automated FPGA sweep as the hBN chip: for each emitter pixel fire a green pump pulse, sample every detector electrode, record which sees a modulated NV-PL response under MW at 2.87 GHz → build the emitter-pixel→qubit-site→detector-electrode lookup table. No camera, no alignment jig. (The only change is the MW frequency in the sweep.)

8. Validation Against Known Studies

8.1 NV⁻ centre — D, ODMR, coherence

  • NV⁻ centre review (D ≈ 2.87 GHz, room-temperature spin): Doherty et al., Phys. Rep. 528, 1–45 (2013) — the canonical reference for the NV centre Hamiltonian and optical cycle.
  • Nanoscale magnetometry with NV⁻ at ambient: Balasubramanian et al., Nature 455, 648–651 (2008).
  • Single-spin coherent oscillations: Jelezko et al., PRL 92, 076401 (2004).
  • Nanoscale magnetic sensing: Maze et al., Nature 455, 644–647 (2008).
  • Near-second coherence under decoupling (T₂ → ~1 s): Bar-Gill et al., Nat. Commun. 4, 1743 (2013).
  • 1.8 ms Hahn-echo T₂ at 300 K in ¹²C-enriched diamond: Balasubramanian et al., Nature Materials 8, 383 (2009) — the basis for the T₂ = 1.8 ms figure used by the QSSC simulator NV profile.
  • NV T₁ at room temperature: Jarmola et al., PRL 108, 197601 (2012).

8.2 CQD green emitter / graphene detector

  • Identical to the hBN base protocol validation (citric-acid+urea CQD synthesis, CQD-LED/ASE, graphene/MoS₂ photodetectors) — see FABRICATION_PROTOCOL.md Section 8. The 532 nm pump / 650–800 nm PL / 650 nm long-pass filter combination is the standard NV-ODMR optical chain, used in every NV reference above.

8.3 Diamond surface preparation

  • Boiling triacid (H₂SO₄:HNO₃:H₂O₂) cleaning and O₂-plasma termination of diamond surfaces are standard NV-surface preparation, consistent with the NV-magnetometry literature above; no single landmark citation is required for the clean itself.
