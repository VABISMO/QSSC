§Executive summary

QSSC (Quantum Solid State Chip) is a complete, open-source quantum platform built around nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centres in the solid state. The development has produced a full hardware stack — two custom printed-circuit boards, the FPGA control RTL, the on-chip firmware and the host software — that addresses two large and complementary application markets: quantum sensing and quantum computation.

Unlike laboratory quantum systems that require dilution refrigerators and rack-sized control electronics, QSSC operates at room temperature on a chip-scale footprint, with 64 independently addressable pixels providing array-level parallelism. The same device that probes magnetic fields, temperature and rotation can execute quantum circuits, generate quantum-safe keys and run ensemble-parallel workloads — one platform, two revenue-relevant domains.

Each application below is set out in three parts: how the platform performs it, what the existing state of the art is, and the concrete advantage QSSC brings over it.

Investment thesis. QSSC compresses a quantum system that normally fills a room into two PCBs and a single FPGA, while keeping the entire stack open and reproducible. That combination — chip-scale, room-temperature, array-parallel, fully open — is the platform's defensible position across both sensing and computation applications.

IQuantum sensing applications

The sensing half exploits the NV centre's optically-detected magnetic resonance: an applied field shifts the NV spin resonance, and that shift is read optically across the 64-channel detector array. The device both drives and reads on-chip, so it is a self-contained, array-parallel quantum probe. Below, each application is detailed alongside the technology it displaces and the specific advantage it offers.

1. Magnetometry & magnetic-field imaging

How QSSC does it. The 64-pixel vector magnetometer reads the full three-component B-vector at every pixel simultaneously, using the 4-axis NV⟨111⟩ orientation: the applied field splits the NV resonance along known crystal axes, and the split magnitude and direction are inverted per pixel. Gradiometric sign-locking rejects common-mode drift (vibration, temperature, ambient fields) by differencing neighbouring pixels, and all 64 channels are read out in one parallel optical frame — no scanning, no sequential acquisition.

Existing solutions
  • SQUID magnetometers — the sensitivity benchmark, but superconducting: they require liquid-helium/dilution cryogenics, magnetic shielding and trained operators, which confines them to laboratories.
  • Optically-pumped magnetometers (OPM) — room-temperature but built from heated vapour cells with bulkier optics; typically single-vector, not an integrated pixel array.
  • Fluxgate / Hall / magnetoresistive sensors — cheap and compact, but one to two orders of magnitude less sensitive than quantum-class probes.
  • Scanning single-NV microscopy — quantum sensitivity, but one NV at a time scanned over the sample with a confocal microscope: slow, and not an array.
QSSC advantage

Quantum sensitivity without the quantum-machine overhead. QSSC delivers NV-class sensitivity at 300 K, chip scale, with 64 parallel channels — no cryogen (vs SQUID), no heated cell or scanning optics (vs OPM / scanning NV), and far higher sensitivity than fluxgate or Hall (vs classical sensors). The array turns a laboratory technique into a field-deployable instrument, and the open stack makes it auditable and reprogrammable.

2. Three-dimensional magnetic source localisation

How QSSC does it. The on-board field-gradient coil — a PCB trace driven by an on-board DAC and current driver — imposes a known spatial gradient across the pixel array. Because the field from a dipole source varies with position in a characteristic way, measuring the per-pixel gradient tensor (field and its spatial derivative across the array) and inverting it yields the three-dimensional position — depth and lateral coordinates — of the source in a single acquisition. The gradient also acts as a spatial reference: a known applied gradient lets the array distinguish a near, weak source from a far, strong one, which is the essence of tensor gradiometry.

Existing solutions
  • Passive gradiometer arrays (fluxgate or SQUID pairs) — measure only the difference between two distant sensors; they infer gradients but do not impose a known encoding gradient, need multiple bulky sensors, and SQUID versions are cryogenic.
  • Magnetic force microscopy (MFM) — high spatial resolution, but a scanning probe operating close to the sample in controlled conditions: slow and unsuitable for field use.
  • Single-sensor magnetometry — recovers source depth only by moving the sensor and fitting over many sequential measurements.
QSSC advantage

Active gradient encoding, not passive differencing. The chip generates its own known gradient on-board and samples it across 64 positions at once, so depth comes out of one measurement instead of a scan or a sensor array. That removes the cryogenics of SQUID gradiometers, the moving parts of MFM, and the multi-instrument cost of fluxgate arrays — while the open-source gradient coil and driver make the whole encoding scheme reproducible.

3. Navigation & geomagnetic sensing

How QSSC does it. The on-chip compass fits the NV resonance doublet against the known crystal axes to return the ambient field's vector, heading, elevation and microtesla-level sensitivity. Because the same array performs gradiometric differencing, it can subtract spatially-uniform interference (vehicle body fields, power lines) and recover heading in environments that defeat ordinary compasses. The geomagnetic mode goes further, matching local field features against a reference map for position fixes.

Existing solutions
  • Magnetoresistive compass chips (AMR/GMR/TMR) — the consumer standard in phones and drones: tiny and cheap, but soft-iron/ hard-iron calibration drifts and sensitivity is limited, degrading heading near vehicles and infrastructure.
  • Fluxgate compasses — better accuracy but larger, power-hungry and costly.
  • Inertial-only navigation (INS) — drifts without a magnetic or GPS reference, which is exactly what a denied environment removes.
QSSC advantage

Quantum sensitivity with built-in interference rejection. NV-class sensitivity plus on-array gradiometry gives heading that survives magnetic clutter where magnetoresistive chips lose calibration — in a chip-scale, room-temperature part. Because heading, temperature and current share the same die, one device can replace a multi-sensor navigation package.

4. Rotation & inertial sensing

How QSSC does it. The platform runs a Ramsey π/2–T–π/2 spin-interference sequence: during the free-evolution window T, the NV phase advances relative to the rotating frame, and rotation of the sensor imprints directly on that phase. Fitting the fringe over T recovers the rotation rate in degrees per second together with its noise floor — a gyroscope whose sensing element is an atomic spin, not a mechanical structure.

Existing solutions
  • MEMS gyroscopes — ubiquitous and cheap, but built from vibrating mechanical structures subject to bias drift, shock sensitivity and ageing.
  • Fibre-optic & ring-laser gyros (FOG/RLG) — navigation-grade, but bulky, power-hungry and expensive.
  • No chip-scale quantum gyro exists in production — spin-based gyros remain laboratory instruments.
QSSC advantage

No moving parts, same chip as the magnetometer. An atomic-spin gyro has no mechanical structure to age or shock (vs MEMS) and no fibre coil or cavity to package (vs FOG/RLG). Integrating it on the same die as the vector magnetometer gives a combined inertial-and-magnetic heading unit from one room-temperature chip — a combination only QSSC's architecture delivers.

5. Thermal & electric-current sensing

How QSSC does it. The NV resonance frequency is intrinsically sensitive to temperature, so each pixel acts as a quantum thermometer: the measured resonance shift maps to local temperature with quantum-limited precision and no electrical contact. In parallel, any current-carrying conductor near the chip produces a field the same pixels measure, so current is recovered from its own field without touching the conductor.

Existing solutions
  • Thermocouples / RTDs / thermistors — contact-based point sensors; IR cameras give contactless maps but are relatively low-resolution, need line-of-sight and calibrate against emissivity.
  • Current shunts — accurate but insert series resistance, dissipate power and break galvanic isolation.
  • Hall / Rogowski current sensors — isolated, but Hall sensors suffer offset drift and Rogowski coils cannot measure DC.
QSSC advantage

Contactless, isolated, multi-modal — one chip. Quantum thermometry gives local, contact-free temperature without emissivity calibration; field-based current sensing gives full galvanic isolation with no shunt and no insertion loss (and DC capability, unlike Rogowski). Crucially, temperature, current and field come from the same 64 pixels in the same acquisition — a multi-modal probe no single-purpose sensor can match.

6. High-resolution & super-resolution imaging

How QSSC does it. Three techniques push spatial information past the native pixel pitch: sub-pixel centroiding reads charge-sharing between detectors to yield 64·S spatial channels; compressed-sensing recovery solves a sparse inverse problem over the gradient-bin dictionary to deconvolve overlapping resonance lines that a naive fit would merge; and quantum-limited matched filtering pulls the weakest signals out of the noise, with lock-in integration improving sensitivity as 1/√N.

Existing solutions
  • SQUID & scanning-NV microscopes — superb resolution but cryogenic or scanning, hence slow and laboratory-bound.
  • Magnetic force microscopy — nanoscale resolution via a scanned probe: slow, near-sample and impractical outside the lab.
  • Conventional pixel arrays — resolution is fixed by the physical pitch; going finer means fabricating more, smaller detectors.
QSSC advantage

Resolution from signal processing and quantum readout, not from smaller fabrication. QSSC gains spatial channels through centroiding and recovers overlapping features through sparse recovery — more resolution from the same 64 pixels, in parallel and at room temperature. That is cheaper and faster than scaling down detector geometry, and it sidesteps the scanning/cryogenic constraints of SQUID and MFM microscopy.

IIQuantum computation applications

The computation half treats the same NV spins as addressable qubits. A Qiskit-compatible control stack compiles circuits into the FPGA's 19 firmware verbs — emitter drive, microwave pulses, readout, gradient control and pulse sequences — executed across the pixel array on real hardware. Each application below is detailed against the technology it competes with.

1. Quantum algorithm execution

How QSSC does it. Users submit circuits as OpenQASM 2 or a Python snippet; a restricted, sandboxed builder (imports limited to qiskit/numpy/math, no file, network or process access, up to 64 qubits) compiles them into the FPGA firmware verbs. The Artix-7 then drives the emitters, microwave pulses and readout on the physical device. Standard algorithms — Bell, QFT, Grover, period-finding, reversible arithmetic and arbitrary user circuits — run end-to-end against real hardware, which is the execution substrate for chemistry simulation, optimisation and machine-learning kernels.

Existing solutions
  • Superconducting qubit machines (IBM, Google, Rigetti) — leading gate fidelities, but require millikelvin dilution refrigerators, heavy control racks and are accessed only via cloud queues.
  • Trapped-ion systems — long coherence and high fidelity, but need ultra-high vacuum, complex laser systems and are physically large.
  • Photonic quantum computers — room-temperature operation but require specialised sources, low-loss networks and detection chains.
  • Simulators — free and instant, but exponential cost limits them to small qubit counts and they are not physical executions.
QSSC advantage

A physical quantum processor you can own and open. QSSC executes circuits on real hardware at room temperature on a chip — no millikelvin fridge (vs superconducting), no vacuum or laser rack (vs trapped ions), no cloud queue or vendor lock-in (vs cloud access), and unlike a simulator it is a genuine physical run. The entire stack is open source, so the device can be inspected, modified and integrated on-site — a property no commercial quantum computer offers.

2. Ensemble-parallel distributed computing

How QSSC does it. Rather than one large entangled circuit, QSSC exploits 64 independent ~10-NV pixels, giving 64× throughput for embarrassingly-parallel workloads. The run-parallel API fans a job across all pixels and calibrate-array first maps each pixel individually, so every one runs at its own measured operating point rather than a nominal average. Sampling, Monte-Carlo and parameter-sweep jobs scale with pixel count instead of circuit depth.

Existing solutions
  • Single large quantum processors — scale by adding qubits to one device, where error rates grow with size and cross-talk couples the components.
  • Cloud quantum fleets — parallelism exists, but as queued access to someone else's machine, with no per-device calibration under your control.
  • Classical HPC — trivially parallel, but without quantum resources for the workloads that need them.
QSSC advantage

Parallelism by construction, with per-pixel calibration. 64 physically separate pixels give linear throughput scaling without growing a single fragile circuit — cross-talk between pixels is minimal because they are independent, and each is calibrated to its own operating point. It is owned hardware (not a cloud queue) running genuinely parallel quantum workloads.

3. Quantum-safe cryptography

How QSSC does it. Two capabilities read directly off the device's physics. Quantum RNG harvests entropy from the AD7616 detector noise floor of a dark channel and derives an Ed25519 keypair with a self-signed X.509 certificate from it — usable cryptographic material straight from a quantum measurement. BB84 quantum key distribution runs the full protocol on the platform, establishing a shared secret whose security rests on the laws of physics rather than computational hardness.

Existing solutions
  • CSPRNGs (AES-DRBG, Hash-DRBG) — the standard, but deterministic: security depends entirely on the secret seed, which is a single point of compromise.
  • Classical hardware TRNGs — non-deterministic but draw on classical noise (thermal, oscillator jitter) whose entropy must be continuously certified and which can be modelled or manipulated.
  • Fibre-based QKD systems — proven security, but deployed as dedicated, point-to-point rack equipment over specialist fibre.
  • Post-quantum algorithms — software-only mitigation whose security still rests on unproven mathematical assumptions.
QSSC advantage

Quantum entropy and quantum distribution on one chip. Keys come from a quantum measurement, not a deterministic generator (vs CSPRNG) and not a classical noise source that must be certified (vs TRNG). BB84 runs on the same device, so entropy generation and key exchange are integrated rather than separate vendors' products — and the whole path is chip-scale and open, unlike rack-mounted fibre QKD.

4. Quantum registers & memory

How QSSC does it. The computation stack is built on registers whose width is a measured, controllable quantity. The on-board field gradient tunes the register width per pixel, so it is calibrated rather than assumed. A carbon-13 nuclear spin serves as a memory qubit that swaps data in and out of the register, holding state longer than the electron spin it backs up. Readout uses 16 exactly-orthogonal prime-period lock-in channels with zero cross-talk, scaling how many lines can be read simultaneously; nitrogen nuclear registers extend the qubit count further.

Existing solutions
  • Superconducting transmons — fast gates but very short coherence: qubit state must be stored in error-corrected codes or lost, and every qubit sits in a millikelvin fridge.
  • Trapped ions — long-lived memory, but each ion is held in an individual vacuum trap with laser addressing: excellent, not scalable or chip-scale.
  • Silicon spin qubits — solid-state and promising, but mostly single/few-qubit devices requiring cryogenics and fab-level control.
QSSC advantage

Longer-lived memory on the same room-temperature chip. A nuclear-spin memory gives a longer-lived store than the electron qubit on the same die — no separate memory technology, no fridge (vs superconducting), no vacuum trap (vs ions). The register width being measured and gradient-tunable rather than fixed at fabrication is a degree of control competing architectures do not expose.

5. Hybrid algorithms & error correction

How QSSC does it. The platform pairs on-chip qubits with a host-side classical loop for the algorithm families that define the quantum roadmap: VQE and QAOA for variational optimisation in chemistry and combinatorics, quantum kernel methods for machine-learning classification, and a small quantum error-correcting code run on the register as the stepping stone toward fault tolerance. The same array additionally supports entanglement-enhanced distributed sensing, where computation and sensing converge.

Existing solutions
  • VQE/QAOA on cloud superconducting hardware — available, but every variational iteration pays cloud round-trip latency inside the optimisation loop.
  • Error correction demonstrations — typically on large cryogenic machines where the overhead of the fridge dominates the experiment.
  • Classical optimisers — strong baselines, with no quantum acceleration available locally.
QSSC advantage

A local, low-latency quantum-classical loop and a path to fault tolerance you can run on the bench. Because the processor is on-site at room temperature, the classical-quantum iterations of VQE/QAOA happen without cloud round-trips, and error-correction experiments run on owned hardware rather than rented cryogenic time. The platform's openness makes each correction step inspectable — the standard by which fault-tolerance progress is judged.

§Platform advantages

The applications above compound into a set of structural advantages over both incumbent quantum hardware and classical instrumentation:

Room temperature

300 K operation — no dilution refrigerator, no cryogenics, no vacuum. The capital and running cost of a quantum system drops from a facility to a benchtop instrument.

Operating cost

Single-chip integration

Drive, microwave, readout, gradient and control live on two PCBs around one die, orchestrated by a single Artix-7 FPGA — a complete product rather than a laboratory setup.

Footprint

Array-parallel

64 independent pixels scale throughput linearly instead of depending on one deep entangled circuit — parallelism without cross-talk.

Throughput

Full-stack hardware

Boards, RTL, firmware and software are designed and implemented in-house — sensing and computation share one device.

Completeness

Qiskit-compatible

Standard OpenQASM/Python circuits lower the barrier from algorithm research to physical execution — no proprietary toolchain.

Adoption

Open source

Boards, RTL, firmware, simulator, API and documentation are published and reproducible — auditable end to end, and free of vendor lock-in.

Trust & ecosystem
Summary. QSSC addresses two application families from one developed platform. Sensing — magnetometry, 3-D field imaging and source localisation, navigation, rotation, thermal and current probing, and super-resolution imaging — at room temperature with array parallelism, displacing cryogenic, scanning or low-sensitivity incumbents. Quantum computation — algorithm execution, ensemble-parallel processing, quantum-safe key generation and QKD, backed by tunable registers, nuclear memory and a roadmap through hybrid algorithms to error correction — as an open, ownable, room-temperature alternative to cloud-only cryogenic machines. One chip-scale, open platform; two markets.
